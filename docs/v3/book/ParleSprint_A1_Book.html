THE EXAM
What TCF Canada tests and where A1 fits

TCF Canada is a French test run by France Éducation international. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) accepts it for some immigration programs. It has four tests:

Be honest with yourself. A1 is the first step, not the goal. Most Canadian immigration programs need about NCLC 7, which is roughly B2. Check the current score tables on the IRCC website. THE SITUATION
You are new in Canada. Before words and grammar, you need the ear and the mouth: how French sounds, how it is written, and how to say names and numbers, because on the phone, in a clinic and in an office, people ask you to spell your name and read out your numbers.

0.1 How French works

French uses the same letters as English, but the letters often make different sounds. The good news: French spelling follows rules, and the rules are very regular. You will learn them one group at a time.

Three big ideas

  • Spelling is not sound. Learn to hear first, then read.
  • The last letter is often silent. petit (small) is said puh-TEE. The t is written, but you do not say it.
  • Every syllable counts. English stresses one syllable (PHO-to-graph). French gives each syllable almost the same length and force, with a small lift only on the last syllable of a group of words.
Tip You already read French every day in Canada: on road signs, packages and shop doors. Now learn how those words really sound.

0.5 u or ou? The rounded vowels

This is the first sound that is really new for English speakers. u and ou are different, and they change the meaning of a word.

  • ou = oo, as in food. Lips round and push forward. tout (all): too.
  • u = ü. Say ee and, without moving your tongue, round your lips as for oo. tu (you): tü.
  • eu = lips round, tongue forward, like her with a rounded mouth. deux (two): duh.

0.7 Consonants and silent letters

Most French consonants are like English ones. The surprises are the silent final letters and a few letter groups.

The big rule: the last consonant is usually silent

petit (puh-TEE), grand (grah(n)), vous (voo), Paris (pah-REE), trop (troh), beaucoup (boh-KOO), chez (shay). The c, r, f, l are often said at the end, so remember CaReFuL: avec (ah-VEK), bonjour (boh(n)-ZHOOR), neuf (nuhf), sel (sel). 0.8 The French r

The French r is not made with the tongue tip like in English. It is made in the back of the throat, a soft friction, a little like clearing your throat very gently or a quiet gargle.

How to find it

  • Say a long ah. Now say g-g-g-ah. Feel the back of your tongue touch the soft palate.
  • Say ha, then make the h rough and soft in the throat: hRa.
  • Keep the tongue tip down, touching your bottom front teeth, and do not move it.

A. être: to be (g01)

être is the most important verb in French. It is irregular, so you learn each form. It says who someone is and what someone is like.

B. The subject pronouns (g04)

Who is acting?

C. tu or vous? (g03)

French has two ways to say you. This is not decoration: using the wrong one can sound rude or strange.

D. -er verbs: parler, habiter, s'appeler (g09a)

Most French verbs end in -er. They are regular. Remove -er, then add the endings:

THE SITUATION
At a café, a corner store, a pharmacy, on a phone bill, with a landlord. Numbers decide what you pay, so numbers are also what the exam checks most in the first questions. This unit makes you confident with them.
